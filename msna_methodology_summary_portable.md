
<h1>Sampling Methodology Summary</h1>
<h3>Multi-Sectoral Needs Assessment — North-West, North-East and North-Central Nigeria, 2026</h3>
<p>This note describes the sampling design used to select households for interview across the Non-IDP and internally displaced (IDP) populations covered by this assessment. It is written as a methodology reference, not an implementation log — it does not describe the software, data-processing steps, or tools used to build the sample, only the statistical design itself and the reasoning behind it. Sections marked <strong>Technical detail</strong> throughout are intended to eventually move into a technical annex; everything else is written for a general, non-technical reader. It is intended as a first, comprehensive draft to be trimmed and adapted into whatever format is needed for the final Terms of Reference or technical annex.</p>
<p>Population groups are defined as <strong>IDPs</strong> and <strong>Non-IDPs</strong> — not "host" — to avoid the ambiguity of a term that, in humanitarian usage, also describes the community an IDP lives embedded within (an "IDP in host community", one of the categories recorded against the IDP population below). Framing the two sampled groups as IDP / Non-IDP avoids relying on an assumption about whether returnees and refugees are or aren't part of "the host community" for sampling purposes — they are simply outside the IDP population as recorded by IOM DTM, and therefore Non-IDP.</p>
<hr>
<h2>1. Sampling frame and stratification</h2>
<p>The assessment covers Non-IDP and internally displaced (IDP) populations across three regions of northern Nigeria — North-West, North-East and North-Central — spanning fourteen states. Non-IDP and IDP populations are treated as two entirely separate sampling frames throughout the design, reflecting both the different population data available for each and, as described in Section 3, a different field procedure for each.</p>
<p>The sampling universe was built from a regular hexagonal grid covering the assessment area, rather than directly from administrative or settlement boundaries. A regular grid gives every part of the landscape an equal opportunity to be represented in the frame and avoids the irregular shapes and highly uneven sizes of using Local Government Area (LGA) or ward polygons as the base sampling unit. Two independent population layers were then attributed to this grid, one per population group:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Non-IDP population</strong> — drawn from a gridded population estimate for Nigeria, giving an estimated number of people and households in each grid cell.</li>
<li><strong>IDP population</strong> — drawn from site-level location data collected under the International Organization for Migration's Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM), which records IDP and returnee sites together with their GPS coordinates and an estimated number of households and individuals present at each. Each recorded site's population was attributed to whichever grid cell contains it. DTM also records a <strong>Population Category</strong> for each site — returnee-classified sites are excluded from the IDP frame entirely (unchanged from the original design); every remaining IDP site is further recorded as either <strong>in-camp</strong> or <strong>in a host community</strong>, which since a 2026-07-28 revision determines which of two different Stage 2 household-selection methods applies to that site (see Section 3).</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Technical detail.</strong> The Non-IDP population layer is WorldPop's constrained gridded population estimate for Nigeria (100m resolution, 2026 projection, release R2025A). A grid cell — for either population layer — is retained in the sampling frame only if its estimated household count exceeds 5 (equivalent to roughly 30 people, using an assumed average household size of 6). Cells at or below this threshold are treated as having no meaningful population and are dropped before stratification; the same threshold is applied identically to the Non-IDP and IDP layers.</p>
<p>Grid cells with no meaningful population under a given layer were excluded from the corresponding frame. As a result, a Non-IDP cluster can only ever be drawn from a cell known to have a resident Non-IDP population, and an IDP cluster can only ever be drawn from a cell containing at least one recorded IDP or returnee site — there is no possibility of a cluster being assigned to an empty or unpopulated location by construction.</p>
<p>Both frames were then stratified by Admin-2 (Local Government Area) and population type together, producing up to two strata per Admin-2 — one Non-IDP, one IDP, wherever both populations are present. Each stratum was treated as an independent sampling domain with its own required sample size, its own selection of clusters, and its own design weight. Stratifying at Admin-2 level, the level the assessment reports findings against, ensures the sample is spread proportionately across administrative units rather than concentrating in a small number of high-population districts.</p>
<p>Before either population layer was gridded, a further exclusion was applied to define the accessible area available for sampling in the first place: an area along Nigeria's international borders, and a set of areas separately assessed as inaccessible, were both removed from the sampling universe entirely (see Section 4 for the exact rule).</p>

<h2>2. Stage 1: primary sampling unit selection</h2>
<p>Within each stratum, the number of clusters required was calculated using a standard two-stage cluster-survey sample size formula, combining:</p>
<ul>
<li>a 90% confidence level,</li>
<li>a conservative assumption of a 50% expected proportion for the indicators being measured (the assumption that maximises the required sample size, and so a safe default where the true proportion is not known in advance),</li>
<li>a 10% margin of error,</li>
<li>a design effect reflecting the extra imprecision introduced by clustering households together rather than sampling them fully at random, based on an assumed intra-cluster correlation coefficient and a cluster size of six households per cluster,</li>
<li>a finite-population correction using each stratum's estimated household population, and</li>
<li>a 10% buffer added to the resulting figure to allow for attrition and cluster-level non-response, before rounding up to a whole number of six-household clusters.</li>
</ul>
<p>Strata with a very small total household population — below the equivalent of six clusters' worth of households — had every eligible grid cell included in the sample directly, rather than a subset being drawn, since the underlying population is too small to sample from meaningfully. All other strata used <strong>probability-proportional-to-size (PPS)</strong> sampling, in which the chance of a given grid cell being selected as a cluster is proportional to its estimated household population — the same measure of size used to build the frame in the first place (the gridded population estimate for Non-IDP strata; the DTM-reported household count for IDP strata). A more densely populated location is therefore proportionately more likely to be selected than a sparsely populated one, which keeps the achieved sample self-weighting toward where most of the population actually lives, while still giving every eligible location in the stratum a non-zero chance of selection.</p>
<p>PPS selection was implemented as a systematic sample with a random starting point: grid cells within a stratum were placed in a fixed order, a random starting point was chosen along the cumulative population total for that stratum, and clusters were then selected at fixed population intervals along that ordering. Because the interval is fixed while the population of individual grid cells varies, a single very large or densely populated cell can occasionally be selected more than once within the same stratum. Where this happened, the repeated selections were combined into a single cluster carrying a proportionally larger household quota, rather than being treated as two separate cluster visits to the same physical location.</p>
<p>A fixed random seed was used for this Stage 1 selection, so that, given the same input population data, the exact set of selected clusters can be reproduced — an important property for an assessment that may need to demonstrate its sample was drawn consistently and without manual interference.</p>
<hr>
<h2>3. Stage 2: household selection procedure</h2>
<p>Stage 2 — selecting which specific households within a chosen cluster are actually approached for interview — was designed and implemented differently for Non-IDP and IDP clusters. This reflects a genuine, deliberate difference in what data is available and reliable for identifying individual households in each context, and is the single most important operational distinction for field teams to understand (see the companion field guide for how this translates into instructions for enumerators).</p>
<h3>Non-IDP clusters: building-footprint household selection</h3>
<p>For Non-IDP clusters, individual building footprints identified from high-resolution satellite imagery were used as a proxy for household locations within the selected grid cell. Footprints were filtered to a minimum detection-confidence threshold and to a plausible residential size range, which excludes both very small non-residential fragments — such as outbuildings, latrines, or storage structures frequently mapped as separate footprints within a single household compound — and unusually large structures unlikely to house a single household. From the resulting set of eligible building locations within a cluster, six households were selected at random, without replacement, as the primary sample, with an equally-sized reserve list — matching the primary target exactly, not a flat number regardless of cluster size — drawn and ranked in order alongside it. This also applies to the minority of clusters formed by more than one repeated PPS draw (see "Repeated cluster selection" below), where both the primary target and its reserve list scale up together. Field teams use this reserve list strictly in rank order whenever a primary household cannot be reached or declines to participate.</p>
<p><strong>Technical detail.</strong> A building footprint is eligible if its detection confidence score is at least 0.75 and its footprint area is between 12m² and 1,000m². The lower bound excludes small non-residential fragments (outbuildings, latrines, storage structures); the upper bound excludes very large structures — warehouses, markets, institutional buildings — unlikely to be a single household's residence.</p>

<p>A small number of selected grid cells contained no building footprint meeting the eligibility criteria at all — typically very sparsely built-up or marginal locations. Rather than leaving these clusters empty, each was reallocated to a different, previously unselected grid cell within the same stratum, drawn using the identical population-proportional selection method as the original Stage 1 draw. This preserves the statistical integrity of the design — the replacement cluster's chance of selection is calculated exactly as any other cluster's would be — while ensuring every cluster in the final sample corresponds to a real, visitable location with an actual population of eligible households to draw from.</p>
<h3>IDP clusters: site-based household selection</h3>
<p>The original design intended to use the same building-footprint method for IDP clusters. This was found, during the course of building the sample, not to work well for IDP locations specifically, for two related reasons.</p>
<p>First, a practical constraint: IDP locations exist in the sampling frame only where they have actually been recorded as populated in the underlying site-level data. Unlike Non-IDP areas, there is no large surrounding pool of unselected grid cells to draw on if a given site's satellite-derived building footprints prove unreliable or unavailable — checking this directly found that, in the large majority of affected districts, every eligible IDP location had already been drawn into the sample, leaving no possibility of a like-for-like substitute of the kind used for Non-IDP clusters.</p>
<p>Second, a data-quality consideration: satellite-derived building footprints are a weaker proxy for individual households in many IDP and informal-settlement contexts than in typical Non-IDP neighbourhoods, since shelter arrangements in such settings can be denser, more temporary, or simply not correspond one-to-one with distinct visible structures from overhead imagery.</p>
<p>For these reasons, household selection for IDP clusters uses the site's own recorded DTM GPS location as the starting point instead of building footprints. Every selected IDP cluster corresponds to a site recorded in the DTM dataset, so a real, field-verifiable location is always available by construction. In a minority of cases, a single selected grid cell corresponded to more than one recorded site nearby; in those cases, the larger site by reported household count was used as the cluster's location, with the presence of the smaller, nearby site retained as supplementary information (<code>n_other_sites_in_hex</code>) rather than discarded.</p>
<p><strong>The method for building the household list from that starting point differs by DTM Population Category</strong> (in-camp vs. dispersed in a host community) — reflecting a genuine operational difference between the two settings, rather than a single method stretched to cover both. Both of the methods below keep to the same underlying design principle used everywhere else in this document: <strong>build a complete household list first, then let the data-collection tool generate the random draw</strong> — enumerator on-the-spot judgement is deliberately avoided wherever a listing is feasible, since the assessment's field-supervision capacity (a single training-of-trainers cascade across 50,000+ planned interviews) was treated as a real constraint on how much discretion any one method could safely leave to an individual enumerator.</p>
<h4>In-camp sites: two-tier listing, with a pre-assigned fallback</h4>
<p><strong>Tier 1 (default, attempted first at every in-camp site).</strong> On arrival at the site's recorded DTM GPS point, field teams construct a complete household list bounded by the visible extent of the camp, which is then entered into the data-collection tool to generate the random selection of six primary households plus an equally-sized ranked reserve list automatically — the reserve list matches the primary target exactly (also scaling together in the minority of clusters formed by more than one repeated PPS draw, see "Repeated cluster selection" below), not a flat number regardless of cluster size. Listing is expected to succeed at the great majority of camps.</p>
<p><strong>Tier 2 (fallback, used only where a field team determines on arrival that a full listing isn't feasible — typically at very large or dense camps).</strong> Selection instead uses a random walk: starting from a fixed point (see backup GPS points below), proceeding in a direction assigned in advance from a randomised bearing table — deliberately <em>not</em> chosen on the spot by the enumerator, consistent with the list-then-RNG-select principle above — and interviewing every <em>n</em>-th household at a fixed interval until reaching six primary households, continuing for a further set number of households beyond the target to form the ranked reserve list using the same interval rule. <strong>The exact interval and reserve-continuation count are still pending field-operations input and should not be treated as finalised values.</strong> The trigger for Tier 2 is the field team's own on-arrival judgement, not a published numeric threshold.</p>
<p><strong>Backup GPS points for random-walk fallback (all in-camp sites).</strong> Every in-camp IDP cluster now has a second, pre-assigned GPS point available for use only if Tier 2 (the random-walk fallback) is triggered on arrival. This was initially generated only for the largest camps, where a camp's DTM-recorded point is often a registration desk, distribution point, or camp management office rather than a location representative of the camp's full extent — a random walk radiating from it risks systematically over-sampling households near that point. Following feedback from partner field teams that Tier 1 (full household listing) feasibility is a broader operational concern than originally anticipated, a backup point was extended to every remaining in-camp cluster as a precautionary measure, so a Tier-2 starting point is always available regardless of camp size. Where a camp's extent could be reliably delineated from satellite imagery, the backup point was randomly placed within that delineated extent; everywhere else, it was randomly placed within a fixed-radius buffer around the DTM point. In both cases, the backup point is for Tier 2 use only — Tier 1 listing should always begin at the primary DTM-recorded point.</p>
<p><strong>Limitation: the Tier 2 fallback is not a full random draw.</strong> Unlike the list-then-RNG method used by default across this entire design, the Tier 2 walk is a systematic rather than simple random sample: it assumes household characteristics aren't meaningfully correlated with distance or direction from the starting point, an assumption that doesn't always hold and can under-represent households at a camp's periphery or in less accessible areas. Because Tier 2 is intended as an infrequent fallback rather than the default, its overall impact on the achieved sample is expected to be limited, but any cluster completed via Tier 2 must be flagged in the dataset so its contribution can be reviewed separately during analysis if needed — this flag is not yet formally specified in the data dictionary and should be added before fieldwork.</p>
<h4>Host-community sites: chief/head-of-settlement listing, bounded by local social recognition</h4>
<p>For IDP sites where the DTM Population Category records households as dispersed in a host community rather than in-camp, there is no single bounded location (like a camp) that can be relied on to contain the great majority of the recorded caseload, so the fixed-radius approach is not used for this group at all. Field teams instead approach the site's chief or head of settlement on arrival and request a full listing of households they recognise as belonging to the displaced population of that settlement — bounded by local social recognition of "belonging to this settlement," not a GPS radius or administrative boundary. Where the settlement comprises multiple distinct named areas or neighbourhoods, teams ask for this listing from a contact within each area rather than from a single informant, and pool the resulting lists before selection. This single additional instruction — asking about sub-areas — is applied uniformly at every host-community site rather than triggered by a separate rule: for a small settlement it collapses back to one informant and one list, while for a large or dense settlement it distributes the listing task across several contacts instead of asking one person to enumerate the whole population. As with the in-camp method, the resulting pooled list is entered into the data-collection tool, which generates the random selection and an equally-sized ranked reserve list automatically — matching the primary target exactly, the same reserve-sizing rule used for Non-IDP and in-camp clusters — rather than leaving the draw to field-level judgement.</p>
<p><strong>Feasibility review behind this design.</strong> Ahead of finalising this method, every host-community IDP site in the sampling frame (1,099 sites) was cross-checked against two feasibility indicators: surrounding Non-IDP population density, drawn from the same gridded density layer used to construct the Non-IDP sampling frame and measured within a fixed radius around the site's GPS point; and the site's own DTM-recorded caseload. Neither measure cleanly separates a distinct "urban" subgroup from the rest — caseload ranges from 5 to 6,077 households (median 79), and surrounding density ranges from near-zero to over 23,000 people/km² (median approximately 1,500), with the two only moderately correlated. Using a combined, deliberately conservative threshold (90th-percentile density <em>or</em> 150+ households), 406 of the 1,099 sites (37%) would be flagged — this figure is contextual, illustrating the scale of variation, not an operational trigger. A stricter combined threshold (95th-percentile density <em>and</em> 200+ households, both conditions required) narrows this to 18 sites, concentrated in Zamfara, Benue, Borno and Adamawa States with no clear pattern distinguishing rural from urban LGAs; these 18 are the figure actually used operationally — flagged for field coordinators to give closer supervision attention and prepare a larger reserve list, using the <em>same</em> method as every other host-community site, not a different one. Given the lack of a clean dividing line between measures, the multi-area listing instruction above — rather than a separate, urban-specific method — was adopted for all host-community sites, so that it scales to this variation without asking field teams to judge in advance which category a site falls into.</p>
<p><strong>Limitation: listing completeness and gatekeeping.</strong> Because host-community household selection still relies on locally-identified informants to compile the list, rather than an independently verifiable structure such as satellite-derived building footprints, households not well known to, or not socially connected with, the informants consulted — including newly arrived, unregistered, or marginalised households — may be under-represented on the list and therefore have a lower chance of selection; informants may also have an incentive to under- or over-report the displaced population present, for reasons unrelated to the assessment. Enumerators are instructed to explicitly ask whether any displaced households are missing from the initial listing before proceeding to selection, as a partial mitigation, not a fix for the underlying risk.</p>
<p><strong>Limitation: PSU attribution.</strong> As with in-camp sites, a host-community IDP site's full recorded caseload is attributed to whichever hexagon contains its recorded GPS point, regardless of how far the settlement's actual households extend beyond that point. This convention applies consistently to both in-camp and host-community sites, but is more visible for host-community sites specifically, where informant-identified households may span a wider and less clearly bounded area than a camp's own footprint would. Field teams are not asked to make any boundary or GIS judgement to resolve this; informants simply list households they consider part of the same settlement by local social recognition, which provides a natural, if imperfect, bound broadly — though not identically — consistent with the hexagon convention used in the design weights (Section 6).</p>

<p>Every IDP record carries the site's DTM <strong>Population Category</strong> group — in-camp or dispersed in a host community (Section 1) — which now directly determines which of the two methods above applies, rather than being recorded for future use only as in the prior revision of this document.</p>
<p>All three approaches — Non-IDP building-footprint draw, IDP in-camp listing (with its walk fallback), and IDP host-community chief-listing — produce the same underlying record structure: one row per planned interview, primary or reserve, with full administrative attribution down to ward level and (once finalised — Section 6) a design weight, so that every record can be analysed together as a single dataset despite having been generated through three different field procedures.</p>

<h2>4. Known limitations and edge cases</h2>
<ul>
<li><p><strong>Small-population strata: excluded, not sampled.</strong> A small number of strata — those with the smallest total household population — are small enough that every eligible location would be included directly rather than a probability sample being drawn, because the underlying population is too small to draw a meaningful sample from. Since a 2026-07-23 revision, such a stratum is only retained in the design if full inclusion of every eligible site — still capped at six households each — can reach the assessment's 10% margin-of-error target; where it can't, the stratum is excluded from the sample entirely (zero clusters, zero interviews) rather than fielded for indicative-only data, since HQ judged that trade-off between operational effort and reporting value not worth making. Under the current population data, all 18 strata small enough to qualify fail this check and are excluded — see Section 5 for the rule, the affected LGAs, and the resulting coverage gap.</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Repeated cluster selection.</strong> In strata where population is concentrated in a small number of very large grid cells, the same cell can be selected more than once by the systematic PPS procedure. Where this occurred, it was treated as a single cluster with a proportionally larger household quota rather than two separate field visits — the methodologically correct way to preserve each unit's true probability of selection under this design. Since 2026-08-04, the reserve list scales with this quota too (reserve = primary target, for every cluster) rather than staying fixed at six regardless of how large the primary target grows — a flat reserve list would otherwise leave a large merged cluster with proportionally the thinnest replacement margin exactly where the most listing/replacement activity happens.</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Zero-eligible-location clusters (Non-IDP).</strong> A small number of Non-IDP clusters had no eligible building footprint at all and were reallocated to a different location within the same stratum, using the same selection method as the original draw (see Section 3). This does not affect the overall stratum sample size or the statistical properties of the design; it only affects which specific location represents that cluster's "slot" in the sample.</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Below-target clusters.</strong> A cluster is flagged as <strong>below-target</strong> (<code>below_target_cluster</code> in the dataset) when fewer eligible households were available in it than its interview target. The consequence of this flag differs by population group, reflecting the different Stage 2 methods (Section 3). For <strong>Non-IDP</strong> clusters, the number of eligible building footprints genuinely limits how many households can be drawn — a below-target Non-IDP cluster's contribution to the sample is capped at whatever was actually available, and it delivers fewer than its full target. For <strong>IDP</strong> clusters, there is no such cap: every IDP cluster's full target number of interview slots is always planned, regardless of the DTM-reported household count at that site, since the actual household list is built by field teams on arrival rather than drawn from a pre-existing pool (Section 3). A below-target IDP cluster therefore still plans its full target — the flag is a heads-up that the DTM estimate on file is lower than that target, worth field-team awareness, not a reduction in planned interviews. In total, 88 clusters out of 5,864 (across both Non-IDP and IDP) are flagged, and are retained in the dataset so they can be reviewed as needed.</p>
</li>
<li><p><strong>Non-IDP strata with a below-target shortfall: identified, corrected.</strong> For every Non-IDP stratum, the calculated target sample size (Section 2) is at least 96 households — the threshold this assessment's validation process treats as a general minimum for stratum-level representativeness. In 28 of the 323 Non-IDP strata, enough of that stratum's clusters were individually below-target that the <em>delivered</em> sample fell short of that target, despite the target itself being set correctly. These 28 span nine states (Benue, Borno, Kebbi, Kogi, Nasarawa, Niger, Plateau, Yobe, Zamfara), most concentrated in — but not limited to — Borno's conflict-affected North-East LGAs (15 of the 28). The most plausible explanation for the concentration in Borno is that satellite-derived building footprints undercount real households in specific conflict-affected areas because structures have been destroyed, abandoned, or not rebuilt since displacement — the same underlying data-quality limitation already noted for IDP sites above; the remaining, more geographically scattered strata reflect the same building-footprint-availability limitation in isolated locations elsewhere. <em>(Revised 2026-08-06: the Niger-buffer change described in Section 4's border-buffer paragraph shifted which specific 28 strata need this correction — Sokoto's Tangaza no longer needs a supplementary cluster, Kebbi's Arewa-Dandi now does — swapping Sokoto for Kebbi in the state list above. The total count (28), the total additional interviews (254 below), and the realized-MoE range (9.03–9.27%) are all unchanged, since both LGAs needed exactly one standard six-household supplementary cluster.)</em></p>
<p>Recalculating the realized margin of error for each of these 28 strata — using the same formula, design effect, and intra-cluster correlation assumption as the original sample-size calculation, but evaluated at the sample actually achieved rather than the sample targeted — found that only 2 of the 28 (Mafa and Ngala) genuinely exceeded the study's 10% margin-of-error threshold before correction; the other 26 were already within tolerance (9.31–9.98%). Rather than apply a uniform fix to a fixed list, every Non-IDP stratum is checked against this same rule (achieved sample below target_sample), and each one that fails it gets the minimum number of brand-new supplementary clusters needed to close its own specific gap — drawn using the identical population-proportional method as every other cluster in the design (see Section 2), at the standard six households per cluster rather than a larger one. Cluster selection probabilities were recalculated for every hexagon in any stratum that received an added cluster, since adding a cluster changes that stratum's effective number of Stage-1 draws; this keeps design weights consistent with the actual number of clusters visited.</p>
<p>This replaces an earlier approach (superseded 2026-07-22) that raised the cluster size from six to seven households for a hand-picked list of 10 conflict-affected LGAs — verified at the time to add 421 additional interviews for those 10 alone. The current approach identifies a broader, more consistently-defined set of 28 strata using the same target-vs-achieved check throughout, and closes all of them for 254 total additional interviews combined. The resulting realized MoE (9.03–9.27% across all 28) sits closer to — while still safely under — the 10% design ceiling than the previous fix's 8.51–8.73% for its 10 LGAs; that gap reflects the previous approach's over-correction (a seven-household cluster size delivered more precision than the design required, at a larger field-burden cost), not a change in what "safely under 10%" means.</p>

</li>
<li><p><strong>IDP Stage 2 field-methodology limitations.</strong> Since a 2026-07-28 revision, IDP household selection uses two different listing methods depending on DTM Population Category (Section 3) — a full listing bounded by the visible camp extent for in-camp sites, falling back to a pre-assigned random walk only where a full listing proves infeasible in the field; and a chief/head-of-settlement listing bounded by local social recognition for host-community sites. </li>

<h2>5. Excluded strata: LGAs too small to sample meaningfully</h2>
<p>As of a 2026-07-23 revision, 18 LGAs' IDP populations are excluded from the sample entirely: zero clusters, zero interviews planned. This is a deliberate HQ-approved design decision, not a data-quality issue or an execution shortfall, and is documented here on its own — separately from the general limitations list — given its direct, LGA-specific implication for what can and cannot be reported.</p>
<p><strong>Why this happens.</strong> A stratum's total household population can fall below the equivalent of six clusters at the standard household-per-cluster target — 36 households — small enough that every eligible site is included directly rather than a probability sample being drawn from it, since the population is too small to sample from meaningfully (Section 2). Stage 2 still caps interviews at six per site rather than fully enumerating every household, and — unlike a PPS stratum falling short of its target — a stratum this small has no supplementary-cluster mechanism available to close the gap (Section 4's "identified, corrected" fix only applies to PPS strata): every eligible site is already included, so there is nowhere left to draw an additional cluster from. Where even that full-inclusion ceiling can't resolve to the assessment's 10% margin of error, the underlying population is judged too small, relative to the assessment's precision target, for a field visit to produce anything better than indicative data. Previously (before 2026-07-23) such strata were still fielded and their findings flagged as indicative; HQ has since decided the trade-off between the field effort of visiting these sites and the reporting value of indicative-only data is not worth it, and these strata are now excluded from data collection outright rather than visited.</p>
<p><strong>The rule.</strong> For every stratum small enough to qualify for full inclusion, the margin of error that doing so would deliver is projected — using the identical formula, design effect, and intra-cluster correlation assumption used throughout this document, evaluated at the maximum achievable sample (every eligible site × six households). Where that projected figure exceeds 10%, the stratum is excluded before Stage 1 cluster selection runs: none of its sites are drawn, so it contributes no clusters and no interviews to the delivered sample. This check is fully generic, not a hand-picked list — it is re-evaluated from each stratum's own population figures every time the sample is built, so a future IOM DTM data refresh that grows the recorded IDP population in any of these 18 LGAs enough to pass the check would make that LGA eligible again (whether fully included or PPS-sampled) without any change to the design itself.</p>
<p>Applying this rule to the current population data finds that <strong>all 18</strong> of the LGAs small enough to qualify for full inclusion fail it — every one is excluded; none remain in the achieved sample. All 18 are IDP strata (no Non-IDP stratum in this design is ever small enough to trigger this check), spanning four states: 14 in Kano, 2 in Niger, and 1 each in Kaduna and Kebbi.</p>

<h2>6. Weighting approach</h2>
<p>A statistically sound design weight will be calculated and applied for every interview, taking into account the sampling methodology described in this document together with the actual, achieved outcomes of data collection — following standard practice for a stratified, two-stage cluster design. The exact weighting formula is not detailed in this document: for IDP records specifically, it depends on the field methodology for identifying households at each site (Section 3). That methodology's broad design is now finalised (two methods, split by DTM Population Category), but specific field-operational parameters within it — chiefly the Tier 2 fallback-walk interval and reserve-continuation count — remain pending, and will directly affect how the within-cluster selection probability is calculated for any IDP interview completed via that fallback. The design-weight columns themselves are not included in the sampling frame files accompanying this document for the same reason, and will be added once those remaining parameters, and the corresponding weighting approach, are finalised.</p>
<p><strong>Coverage gap from excluded strata (Section 5).</strong> Whatever the final weighting approach, one point holds regardless: the 18 excluded IDP strata contribute no records to the dataset at all, so no weighting adjustment can recover their population share in any state- or regional-level IDP aggregate — this is a genuine coverage gap, not something a weighting scheme corrects for. See Section 5 for detail.</p>

<h2>8. Partner coverage: an operational layer on top of the design frame</h2>
<p>Sections 1–7 above describe the complete sampling <strong>design</strong> — every LGA and stratum the design would field, entirely unaffected by this section. As of a 2026-07-30 revision, a separate, additive <strong>operational coverage layer</strong> sits on top of that design frame: which of these LGAs a partner has actually confirmed they can cover on the ground. This does not change the design frame itself, its strata, its sample sizes, or the 52,754-interview total in Section 7 — that figure remains the correct answer to "what does the approved design call for." This section answers a different question: "of that design, what can actually be fielded given confirmed partner coverage today."</p>
<p><strong>Mechanism.</strong> A partner-coverage list (one row per LGA, per region, recording whether at least one partner has confirmed coverage) was joined onto the design frame by LGA. Because the coverage source had no admin-2 pcodes, matching was done by state and LGA name, cross-checked against the frame's own pcodes; 269 of 323 LGAs matched cleanly, 10 matched via a manually-reviewed name-variant reconciliation (e.g. "Otukpo" → Oturkpo, "Munya" → Muya — typos, alternate spellings, and hyphenation differences, all within the same state), and all 44 Kano State LGAs were absent from the coverage source entirely — confirmed by the assessment team as a genuine "no partner covers Kano" position, not a data gap, and treated identically to any other explicitly-declined LGA. Two fields were added to both the Sampling Frame and Strata-Level Summary: <code>coverage_status</code> ("covered" / "not_covered") and <code>exclusion_reason</code> — "none", or one or both of <code>partner_coverage_declined</code> and <code>certainty_stratum_below_moe_threshold</code> (the small-population exclusion from Section 5 — the field name is retained from the underlying data build, though this document describes it in plain terms), joined together where an LGA is affected by both simultaneously. These two exclusion reasons are genuinely independent: coverage applies at the whole-LGA level (both Non-IDP and IDP strata together), while the small-population reason applies only to a specific LGA's IDP stratum, so a stratum can fail one, the other, both, or neither.</p>
<p>The design frame itself (Sections 1–7, the <strong>FULL</strong> frame) is preserved unchanged, with these two columns added, so it remains fully re-derivable if coverage is reconfirmed again later — no part of the design needs rebuilding, only the coverage join needs re-running. A <strong>WORKING</strong> frame — the subset where <code>coverage_status</code> = "covered" <em>and</em> <code>exclusion_reason</code> = "none" — is what is actually planned to be fielded today.</p>

<p><strong>Overlap between the two exclusion reasons.</strong> 17 LGAs nationally are excluded for both reasons at once — their IDP stratum was already excluded under the small-population rule (Section 5), and separately no partner covers the LGA at all. This overlap is <strong>IDP-only</strong>: no Non-IDP stratum is affected by both exclusion reasons at once, since the small-population rule (Section 5) never applies to Non-IDP strata. Structurally, the overlap is dominated by Kano: once Kano was confirmed fully declined by any partner (Mechanism, above), all 14 of its already-excluded IDP strata became double-excluded by construction, so as Kano coverage is confirmed or revised in future, this count should be expected to move together with it.</p>
<p><strong>Reporting guidance.</strong> As with the small-population exclusion gap (Section 5), an LGA excluded for coverage reasons should be reported as a coverage gap, not a precision caveat — no indicator of any kind, indicative or otherwise, can be produced for an LGA with zero interviews planned. Unlike Section 5's exclusion, this one is not a fixed feature of the design: it reflects partner coverage confirmed as of 2026-07-30 and is expected to be revisited as coverage is reconfirmed or partners come online in new LGAs, which is exactly why the FULL frame (Sections 1–7 plus the two new columns) is kept intact rather than discarding excluded rows outright — re-deriving an updated WORKING frame from a coverage refresh is a filter, not a rebuild.</p>
